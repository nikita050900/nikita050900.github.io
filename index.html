I find weak signals in noisy data.

Bayesian inference at scale, model validation, and the checks that separate a real detection from a bad sensor.

Try the 60-second demo Why this transfers

My PhD uses 15 years of timing data from 68 pulsars, some of the most precise clocks in the universe, to search for a faint periodic signal from pairs of supermassive black holes. No single pulsar shows it. The signal only appears when all of them are modeled together, which means the real work is statistical: billions of MCMC samples, competing noise models, and a lot of care about what counts as evidence.

I finish in August 2027 and am looking for full-time roles in quantitative research, model risk and validation, statistical science, or scientific computing, anywhere in the US.

What the problem feels like

A toy version of my day job. The dashed curve is the signal we are looking for. The solid trace is what a detector actually records: the signal buried in correlated noise. Drag the slider to change how loud the signal is, then switch from one channel to all 68 combined.

0.0σconsistent with noise

Combining channels grows the statistic roughly as √N when the noise is independent. In real data it is not independent, which is where the interesting statistics start.

Applying a constraint after sampling instead of before

A post-processing mask on stored MCMC samples, shown to be importance reweighting, plus the prior correction that keeps the estimator stable.

Is the detection real, or is it one bad sensor?

Leave-one-out influence, permutation-style null tests, and a diagnostic for how posterior support moves with frequency.
